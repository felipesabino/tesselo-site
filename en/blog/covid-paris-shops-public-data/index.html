Covid in Paris: what public data kept of it

Fewer bars, more couriers, postponed failures, twice as many bikes: ten charts from five public sources on Paris shops during and after Covid.

The first lockdown began on 17 March 2020. Shops classed as non-essential reopened for good on 19 May 2021. Between those two dates the public registers kept recording: every opening, every closure, every court ruling. Five years on, here is what they kept of it, in Paris.

A sudden drop, then the recovery

In 2020 Paris recorded 4,189 shop openings against 5,424 the year before, 23% fewer. The surprise is the other line: closures fell too, by 11%. Public aid, deferred rents, courts running slowly: in 2020 few shops opened, but few closed. By 2021 openings were climbing again (5,195), and 2022 passed 2019.

Who slowed down, who sped up

Bars and cafés (−47%) and restaurants (−34%) lead, as expected: they stayed shut longer than anyone else. Three lines are less expected.

  • Pharmacies, −31%. They never closed. But the number of pharmacies is capped by law: an “opening” there is nearly always a takeover, and sales of businesses dropped in 2020 (see below).
  • Dry cleaners and laundromats, +27%. The common type most clearly up, on small numbers (51 openings, then 65). Nurseries rose too (68, then 76).
  • Hairdressers, only −10%. Closed during the lockdowns, they still opened almost as many salons as in 2019, while clothing, closed on the same dates, lost 26%.

Among rarer types the numbers are too small for a chart, but they point the way lockdown did: bikes and sports (26 openings in 2019, 39 in 2020), hardware stores (15, then 25), bookshops (25, then 31).

Geography does not tell a simple story. The drop reaches 19 arrondissements out of 20, from the 4th (−40%) to the 7th (−11%), with no clear split between centre and edge, or between office and residential districts. The 1st is the only one up, from 128 to 133 openings.

Two years later: who came back

In 2022 the total passed that of 2019, but not with the same shops. Caterers (+56%) and beauty salons (+31%) open far more than before; grocery shops (+11%) and fast food (+8%) follow. Bars and cafés did not come back: 27% fewer openings than in 2019. Nor did clothing (−18%).

Two types are missing from this chart: hobby schools and fitness studios. Our count keeps only establishments that declare a shop sign, and from 2021 sports and leisure classes declare one four to five times more often than before (8% in 2020, 33% in 2021 for cultural teaching). Their apparent rise, +90% and +66%, therefore comes mostly from the register. Counting every registration, the rise between 2019 and 2022 is 26% for cultural teaching and 18% for sports teaching.

Away from the shopfront: couriers and online shops

The SIRENE register holds more than shops. Across every kind of business, registrations in Paris fell only 5% in 2020 (104,615 against 109,699), while shop openings fell 23%. Some activities even had their best year.

  • 7,909 couriers and delivery riders registered in Paris in 2020, against 4,539 in 2019. That is more than three times the number of restaurants, fast food outlets and bars opened the same year (2,272). Meals kept moving, by bike.
  • Online shops gained 57% in a year and have not slowed since: 835 registrations in 2019, 7,186 in 2025.
  • The less visible losers: tourist guides and booking services (−44%), taxi and ride-hailing drivers (−33%), trade fair organisers (−25%). Their customers were tourists and business travellers.
  • Computer shops, −49%, in the year everyone equipped themselves to work from home. The purchases went online.

The courier peak has a sequel: 7,403 of these establishments closed in 2023, then more than 7,500 a year. Many of those registrations lasted only as long as the demand.

Opening in 2020: a bad bet?

Shops opened in 2020 held up at least as well as the others: 24.6% closed within three years, against 25.9% for those of 2019 and 27 to 31% for earlier years. There were fewer of them, aid covered their first months, and those who signed a lease in the middle of lockdown probably had a solid project. This is not proof, it is a signal: opening against the tide was not penalised.

Failures, postponed more than avoided

BODACC, the official bulletin where commercial courts publish their decisions, shows the same mechanism. In 2021, at the height of state-guaranteed loans and the solidarity fund, liquidations fell to 531, a quarter fewer than in 2019. Then they climbed: 783 in 2022, 885 in 2023, the highest of the series. 2023 is also when shop closures reached their peak.

Sales of businesses tell the other half: 2,310 notices in 2019, 1,628 in 2020, 30% fewer. Those who wanted to sell waited, and so did those who wanted to buy.

What stayed in the street

Three other public sources still bear the mark of the period. None of them isolates the effect of Covid: they show what changed around it.

Cycling doubled. 51,800 Parisians cycled to work in the 2017 census, 111,100 in that of 2023. The temporary lanes of May 2020 became permanent. Public transport lost 43,800 daily users and the car 24,800. For a shop, the people passing the window no longer move at the same speed.

Summer terraces were born of Covid. When lockdown lifted in 2020, the city let cafés and restaurants set up on parking spaces for free. In 2021 the permit became a category of its own, from spring to autumn. It now counts 4,975 permits for 53,000 m²: more than one Paris terrace in four.

Births were falling before; they have fallen faster since. Paris lost about 450 births a year between 2010 and 2019. Since 2019 it is about a thousand a year: 27,373 births in 2019, 21,189 in 2025, 23% fewer. Covid does not explain all of it, but the change of slope dates from then. For a nursery or a toy shop it is a longer-lasting signal than the lockdowns.

What these figures do not say

  • A date in a register is not a cause. This data shows what happened during and after Covid, not what Covid alone brought about.
  • Closures reach the register late, and sometimes never. Openings are dated more reliably.
  • A takeover counts as one closure and one opening. “Openings” of pharmacies, bakeries or bars are often a change of operator.
  • A declared shop sign is not a shop. Our count leaves out self-employed people with no sign. When an activity starts declaring more of them, as sports and leisure classes have since 2021, its “openings” rise without there being more places.
  • Business address providers are left out. 97 addresses where hundreds of unrelated companies are registered without trading there (6,326 establishments) are not counted as shops.
  • Small numbers move a lot. The charts by type keep only the types with at least 50 openings in 2019.
  • BODACC notices cover here only the companies matched to a closed shop, since late 2016. Sole traders rarely appear in them.
  • The census is an average over five years of surveys: the 2023 one mixes answers from 2021 to 2025.
