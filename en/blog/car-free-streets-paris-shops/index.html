939 streets where cars give way: what the city’s files say about their shops

Pedestrian areas, school streets, shared streets: 939 Paris streets laid over the register of shops. One map, six charts, nearly one shop in five, and a before-and-after comparison that shows no visible gap.

In Paris, a street closed to cars leaves a written trace: an order, published in the city’s official bulletin. The city keeps two open files of them, pedestrian areas and shared streets, which add up to 939 streets or groups of streets, most with the date of their order. We laid them over the register of shops. What comes out fits in three findings: nearly one Paris shop in five is on one of these streets, the pace of orders changed in 2020, and the register shows no visible gap between these streets and their neighbours in the three years after the order.

Three kinds of street

  • 521 pedestrian areas, from the Montorgueil quarter to a dead end a few metres long.
  • 190 school streets: pedestrian areas in front of a school, often short.
  • 228 shared streets: cars still pass, at 20 km/h at most.

2020, the year the pace changes

Until 2019, the city dates between 3 and 20 streets a year, with two exceptions: 35 in 2009 and 41 in 2013. Then come the school streets: none before 2020, 45 that year, 40 in 2021. Since 2024, the three kinds together make at least 70 orders a year.

The 2013 peak says something else. That year, 31 shared streets receive their order at once: Aligre, the Butte-aux-Cailles, Rue du Commerce, the Gaîté. These are not 31 building sites, they are paperwork put in order. We come back to it below, because it is the main limit of these dates.

Nearly one shop in five

10,900 open shops have their address on one of these streets: 18.1% of the shops of Paris. They are very unevenly spread. The Montorgueil-Saint-Denis quarter alone has 549, Les Halles 426, Beaubourg 198. At the other end, 163 streets have no shop at all, and the median street has 4 for a pedestrian area or a school street, 10 for a shared street.

What is sold there

In a pedestrian area, people eat and drink: restaurants, fast food, bars and cafés make up 53.8% of shops, against 44.7% in all Paris. Market trades are more present too, greengrocers and fishmongers first, a reminder that many of these streets are old market streets. Services are rare: about half as many dry cleaners and fitness studios.

School streets look like what their name suggests: more hobby schools, nurseries and bookshops than elsewhere, less clothing. They are also the streets where chains weigh least.

Before and after the order: no visible gap

The question everyone asks is about the effect: does a street given back to people on foot gain shops? The public files allow a simple comparison. For the 193 pedestrian areas and shared streets dated from 2012 to 2023 that had at least one shop, we counted openings and closures in the three years before the order, then in the three years after. And we made the same count for the shops nearby, between 20 and 250 metres from the street.

The two series move together. On the street, openings go from 9.8 to 10.2 per 100 shops a year; nearby, from 9.9 to 10.6. Closures rise by 0.6 points on both sides. Street by street, the result is the same: 77 streets gained shops faster after the order than before, 75 more slowly.

One detail stands out. On these streets, the new shops are no longer the same: the share of clothing among openings falls from 18.6% to 14.2%, that of fast food rises from 15.3% to 20.2%. But the movement exists nearby too (clothing from 12.5% to 10.7%, fast food from 16.2% to 18.4%): it is stronger on the street, it is not specific to it.

For school streets, the gap leans slightly the other way: the number of shops grows by 6.8% in the three years after the order, against 10.5% nearby. But these are 85 streets and 560 shops, all dated from 2020 to 2023: too few, and too close to Covid, to conclude anything.

Why this “nothing” proves nothing

  • The date is that of a paper, not of a building site. The Montorgueil quarter, pedestrian since the 1990s, carries the date 2013; Les Halles, pedestrian for longer still, that of 2016. For these streets, “before” and “after” compare two periods when the street was already pedestrian.
  • 249 streets out of 939 have no date, of which 205 pedestrian areas. We looked for another way to date them, without success: no other public file gives the year. They are not the large streets of the centre (12% are in the first six arrondissements, against 31% of dated areas) but smaller streets, with a few well-known market streets: Rue Daguerre, Rue de Lévis, Rue des Martyrs.
  • A street is not chosen at random. The city often pedestrianises a street that already has shops and people. Comparing with the streets nearby corrects part of this bias, not all of it.
  • Three years is short, and for the orders of 2020 to 2023 those three years fall on Covid and what followed.
  • The register counts shops, not their sales. A street can keep the same number of shops and change rents, customers or takings with nothing showing here.
  • Closures reach it late, and sometimes never: those of recent years are undercounted, on the street as nearby.

What public data does not contain

  • The number of people on foot. The city publishes counts of cars, lorries, bikes and scooters. No open file counts pedestrians: saying that a pedestrian street “draws more footfall” cannot be checked with public data.
  • Changes of traffic plan. A street cut for through traffic or made one-way is neither a pedestrian area nor a shared street: it is in neither file. We looked this way for a street of the 17th arrondissement that a reader told us was closed to cars: it is not listed, and the nearest traffic sensors are on the main roads around it, not on the street.
  • Streets reopened to cars. The files describe the streets in force today, not those that stopped being so.
  • Operations without a permanent order: streets closed on Sundays, summer terraces, works.
