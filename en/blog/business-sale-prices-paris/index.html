What a business sells for in Paris

From €20,000 for a beauty salon to €800,000 for a pharmacy: what 5,254 business sales published in BODACC since 2023 say about prices in Paris, by type of shop and by arrondissement.

Taking over an existing shop starts with paying a price to the person leaving. That price is not posted anywhere on the street, but it is public: every sale of a business is published in an official gazette, with its amount. We read the Paris notices since January 2023. Here is what a business sells for in Paris, by type of shop and by arrondissement.

The business, the premises, the lease: three different things

  • The business (in French, the fonds de commerce) is the activity itself: the customers, the shop name, the equipment, any licences, and the right to occupy the premises (the “droit au bail”, the right to the lease). It is what you buy when you “take over” a bakery or a hair salon.
  • The premises (the “walls”) are the unit itself. They usually belong to someone else, to whom the shopkeeper pays rent.
  • The lease is the contract between the two. It passes on with the business.

Every price in this article is the price of a business: neither the premises nor the rent are in it.

Where these prices come from

BODACC is the French state’s official gazette of civil and commercial notices. When a business is sold, the buyer must have a notice published there: it warns the seller’s creditors, who can then come forward before the price is paid to the seller. The notice states the price in full: “business acquired by purchase at the stated price of 225,000 euros”.

We read 7,436 Paris sales with a price, published from January 2023 to early November 2026. To find the type of shop, we matched the buyer and the seller to the shops in INSEE’s SIRENE register: 5,254 sales, 71%, belong to one of the types Pignon follows. Everything below is about those 5,254 sales. We keep only the type, the arrondissement, the month and the price: no name, no address.

Half of all businesses sell for less than €145,000

The median price is €145,000: half of the businesses sold for less, the other half for more. The middle half lies between €50,000 and €300,000. Almost one sale in four (23%) closed under €50,000, and 252 sales (5%) at a million or more. Such a spread mostly says that there is no average “Paris shop”: the price depends first on what is sold.

From one type of shop to another: 1 to 40

A pharmacy sells for a median €800,000, a beauty salon for €20,000: forty times less. Between the two, three groups stand out.

  • The expensive ones: pharmacies, bakeries, bars and cafés. The number of pharmacies is capped by law, so almost none open: one is bought. A bakery (€390,000) sells with its bakehouse, a bar or café (€300,000) with its licence and often its terrace.
  • The middle: butchers (€220,000) and restaurants (€170,000). Restaurants, fast food included, alone account for 53% of matched sales (2,779). Florists and pet shops, cheese and fine food shops, and opticians follow closely, between €140,000 and €145,000.
  • The affordable ones: hairdressers (€46,000) and beauty salons (€20,000). These are frequent sales too: 535 for beauty salons, the second most sold type. A quarter of those salons changed hands for €10,000 or less. Little equipment, small premises, customers attached to the person more than to the place: the business is worth little, which is no judgement on the trade.

Other types have too few sales for the chart. As an indication: €130,000 for wine shops (24 sales), €100,000 for bookshops (25 sales), €40,000 for cobblers (16 sales).

The median does not say it all: the spread within one type

Two lines are worth a pause.

  • Grocery shops: from €30,000 to €350,000. The top of the range is almost twelve times the bottom. Under one word, the register files the neighbourhood food shop, the mini-market and the supermarket: these are not the same markets. The median (€76,000) describes none of them.
  • Bakeries: from €220,000 to €590,000. One of the tightest ranges on the chart, under three times; only dry cleaners and laundromats are tighter (€52,000 to €122,000). A Paris bakery rarely sells for little: only one in ten went for under €78,000.

For a founder the signal is simple: in some trades the median gives a reliable order of magnitude; in others (grocery shops, cheese and fine food shops) it hides very different businesses, and only the range means something.

The arrondissement matters, but less than the trade

From the 1st (€280,000) to the 12th (€100,000), the gap is almost 1 to 3: far less than the 1 to 40 between types. And this chart mixes two effects, the price of the area and the shops that are sold there. An arrondissement where many beauty salons and small grocery shops change hands will have a low median, even if its restaurants sell high. The 14th is an example: €130,000 all types together, among the five lowest medians, but €170,000 for its restaurants, as much as the 10th or the 5th and as much as the Paris median for restaurants.

To separate the two effects, one has to look at a single type. Restaurants are the only one with enough sales for that.

For the same trade the gap narrows a little: from €300,000 in the 1st to €125,000 in the 17th. The 1st aside, every arrondissement sits between €125,000 and €230,000, less than one to two, and ten out of twenty between €150,000 and €170,000. The type of shop therefore weighs far more on the price of a business than the address does, at least at the scale of the arrondissement.

About 1,400 sales a year, at a stable price

Over a full year there were 1,416 sales in 2023, 1,367 in 2024 and 1,380 in 2025. The median price moved little: €140,000, €145,000, then €150,000. Over the first ten months of 2026 the number of sales is 10% below that of 2025 (1,054 against 1,165), with a median of €150,000. It is a signal to watch, not yet a trend: the drop covers ten months only.

What these figures do not say

  • Almost three sales in ten are missing. 29% of the sales we read are matched to no type: activities Pignon does not follow (offices, services…), companies not found at the address, or companies holding shops of different types at the same postcode, which we leave out rather than guess.
  • Company takeovers do not appear. Many takeovers are done by buying the shares of the company that runs the shop, not the business itself. No sale notice is then published, and no price.
  • Small shops are under-represented. Sole traders and craftspeople publish less often. The real prices of the smallest businesses are probably lower still.
  • Some types share their sales. The register does not tell a bar from a café, a dry cleaner from a laundromat, nor a florist from a pet shop: they are counted together. Restaurants include fast food (881 sales out of 2,779).
  • The published price is not the cost of taking over. It includes neither the stock, nor the fees, nor the works, nor the lease deposit. And it says nothing of the floor area, the rent or the turnover: two businesses at the same price may have nothing in common.
  • Sales under €1,000 and over €10 million are left out: token prices between related companies, or groups of shops sold as one.
  • No detail below the arrondissement. A neighbourhood has too few sales, and a single sale could point to one shopkeeper.
