The rules of a street: what Paris open data says before signing a lease

Streets protected by the city plan, terraces, markets, planning filings, Sunday opening: two maps and seven charts from five Ville de Paris files on what a shop may do on a street.

Before signing a lease, founders visit the premises, count passers-by and look at the neighbours. The rules of the street itself get less attention: what the city plan protects there, what the pavement allows, the days a market sets up in front of the window, the works that must be filed, the right to open on Sundays. In Paris, the city publishes all of this as open data. Here are five files, what each one says, and what it does not.

1. Is the street protected for shops?

The PLU (plan local d’urbanisme, the city’s planning rules, whose “bioclimatic” version was voted on 20 November 2024) draws lines along certain streets. On those lines, a ground-floor shop cannot be turned into a home or an office. For a founder, it is a signal about the street more than about the premises: the neighbouring shopfronts are meant to stay shopfronts.

The file holds 5,185 lines, or 361 km of protected streets. It has three levels:

  • shops and crafts, the general case: 323 km, 89% of the total;
  • craft shops, lines aimed at crafts in particular: 29.5 km;
  • cultural shops (bookshops, galleries…): 9.2 km, of which 7.0 km in the 5th and 6th arrondissements alone. Thirteen arrondissements have none.

Protection follows the shopping streets, not the size of the arrondissement. In kilometres, the large arrondissements lead: 32.5 km in the 15th, 30.7 km in the 11th. Set against area, the order changes.

The 6th has 10.1 km of protected streets per km², ahead of the 10th (8.6) and the 11th (8.5). The 15th, first in kilometres, falls below the Paris average (3.9 against 4.2). At the bottom come the 7th (1.2) and the 16th (1.6): woods aside, the 16th is three and a half times the size of the 6th (7.6 km² against 2.1 km²) and has about half as many protected streets (12.0 km against 21.6 km). The centre is not uniform: 7.4 km per km² in the 2nd, 2.3 in the 4th, which has only 3.7 km of protected streets.

The city publishes this file “for information, without regulatory value”. For a given address, the official city plan and the city’s planning office are the authority.

2. Can a shop use the pavement?

A terrace or a display on the pavement needs a permit from the city. Two points matter before taking over premises. First, in Paris this permit is personal and revocable: it does not pass with the business, and the new operator must apply again. Second, it depends on the width of the pavement and on the city’s terrace rules: a terrace next door does not guarantee one for the shop beside it.

The register holds 13,396 year-round terraces, 4,975 summer terraces (spring to autumn, born of Covid: see our post on that period) and 4,690 displays. What stands out is their size.

The typical Paris terrace fits on 6.6 m²: that is the median for year-round terraces, roughly one row of tables along the window. More than one in three (37%) is under 5 m², and only one in ten is 20 m² or more. Summer terraces are a little larger (median 8.5 m²), displays smaller (3.8 m²).

The 10th (1,377 terraces) and the 11th (1,320) lead. The share of summer terraces varies twofold: 18% in the 1st and the 5th, 36% in the 8th, 38% in the 7th, against 27% for Paris as a whole. The 8th has more summer terraces (397) than any other arrondissement.

Terraces are not only for cafés. Of the 8,394 shops we could link to a terrace permit, 82% are restaurants. But there are also 328 bakeries, about 17% of the bakeries open in Paris, and more than wine shops, cheese shops and grocery shops put together (203). These figures are floors: a permit filed under the company’s head-office number, or under an old number, escapes us.

3. Is there a market at the door, and on which days?

A market is not a rule, but it changes a street on the days it is held: stalls in front of the windows, more customers or more competitors depending on what the shop sells. The city’s file lists 80 open-air markets, 72 of them selling food (4 of those organic), plus flower, flea, stamp and craft markets.

No open-air food market is held on a Monday. Saturday is the busiest day (31 markets), and the other days are close to each other, between 22 and 27. The rhythm is nearly always the same: 56 markets out of 72 are held two days a week. Their stalls laid end to end make 26.8 km, with a median of 289 m per market; the longest is over a kilometre (1,182 m).

The spread is uneven: 8 food markets in the 13th, 7 in each of the 12th, 15th and 16th, none in the 3rd or the 7th (covered markets aside).

4. Which works go through the city, and how long does it take?

The city publishes the planning filings it receives: decisions of the last six months and filings under review. We keep three kinds, from the words of their description, refused filings left out: 1,199 filings for shopfront works, 592 changes of use (moving from one use to another, an office to a shop for instance; 271 name a shop) and 531 street-side façade cleanings.

What the file records, without our saying here what the law requires: 1,118 of the 1,199 shopfront filings are prior declarations (déclarations préalables), the lightest kind of filing. Changes of use more often go through a building permit (346 of 592). The difference shows in the time it takes.

Two to three months for most declarations, more than five for most permits. Of the prior declarations filed in August, 78% were granted by early November; of those filed in July, 94%. For building permits, none of those filed since August is granted, and fewer than half of those filed in May (46%, of 28 filings). Nearly one permit under review in two (48%) has been waiting for more than 90 days, against 5% of declarations.

For a project, the signal is simple: shopfront works and a change of use of the premises do not run on the same calendar. The city’s planning office says which filing applies to an address.

5. Can a shop open on Sundays?

Paris has 12 international tourist zones, set by ministerial orders in 2015 and 2016. Shops there are allowed to open on Sundays and in the evening until midnight. The outlines are drawn street by street.

5% of the area of Paris, one shop in seven. The twelve zones cover 571 hectares, 5.5% of the city, woods included. Yet they hold 8,840 of the 60,189 open shops we count (14.7%), and more than one clothing shop in three (1,748 of 5,158, or 34%). Their sizes do not compare: nearly 1,700 shops at Les Halles, 58 at Saint-Émilion Bibliothèque.

A zone is a legal outline, not a measure of footfall. It says what is allowed, not what the neighbouring shops do or how many visitors come.

What the figures do not say

  • The area of an arrondissement is the sum of IGN’s neighbourhood outlines, without the Bois de Vincennes and the Bois de Boulogne. Parks, railway land and the Seine are still counted.
  • A file is not the rulebook. The protected-street lines are published for information. For an address, the official city plan and the city’s planning office are the authority.
  • A terrace permit does not prove the terrace is in use, and the area is the declared one. Terraces are linked to shops through the establishment number (SIRET): the figures by type are floors.
  • The markets file dates from April 2024 and has no covered markets. A market moved or closed since may still be in it.
  • Planning filings are a rolling window of six months, with no decision date. Times are read backwards: the share of filings already granted by month of filing, refusals left out. The kinds of filing are recognised from the words of their description, and façade cleanings that do not name the street side are missing.
  • The shops counted are those of the SIRENE register for the types Pignon follows, without sole traders with no shop sign and without the addresses of business address providers, where hundreds of companies share an address without trading there. Bars and cafés form one type.
  • A filing is not a building site: authorised works are not always carried out.
  • The tourist zones date from 2015 and 2016. Opening on Sundays also depends on other rules, for employees in particular, which this file does not describe.

And on your street?

Pignon reads these same files for a neighbourhood or an address: the metres of street protected by the city plan, the terrace permits within 20 m, the markets a few minutes’ walk away and their days. These are signals, with the number and the source behind each one.
