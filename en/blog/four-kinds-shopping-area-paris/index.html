Four kinds of Paris shopping area: what grouping streets by their shops shows

Paris cut into 879 squares of 250 metres, grouped from their shops alone: fashion streets, going out, everyday food shopping, neighbourhood services. One map, seven charts, and why four groups hold when six do not.

A street is called a “shopping street”, as if there were only one kind. We put the question to the data: if Paris is cut into 250-metre squares, and the squares whose shops look alike are grouped together, with the computer told nothing about arrondissements or neighbourhoods, how many kinds come out? The answer is four groups, which draw a map any Parisian will recognise. And one warning: beyond four, the groups no longer hold.

Nobody drew a border on this map. Each square was classed from its shops alone, and yet the groups form blocks: the centre, the west, the north-east, and, around the centre, a ring running from the Latin Quarter to the 9th and the 11th arrondissements.

How to read the groups

Everywhere in Paris, restaurants and fast food dominate: together, four shops in ten. What sets a group apart is therefore not its most frequent type, but what it has more of, or less of, than the average. The charts below compare the share of a few types in the group with their share across all squares.

1. Fashion streets: the centre and the main boulevards

One shop in six sells clothes there (17.0%, against 8.9% on average). It is the densest group, with 81 shops per square (median), and the one where chains weigh most: 24.0% of shops. Everyday food shops are rare: about a third as many butchers and greengrocers as the average.

All the shops of the 1st and the 3rd arrondissements, or nearly all, are in this group, as are 93% of the shops of the 2nd, 92% of the 4th and 65% of the 6th and of the 8th.

2. Going out: restaurants, bars, books

This is the largest group by number of shops (29.0%). Restaurants, fast food and bars make up more than one shop in two (52.4%). The most over-represented type is less expected: bookshops, one and a half times as present as elsewhere. Clothing, on the other hand, is rarer than average (5.5% against 8.9%): that is what separates this group from the fashion streets, although both have as many restaurants.

All the shops of the 5th, or nearly all, belong to it, as do 69% of the shops of the 11th, 63% of the 9th and 49% of the 10th.

3. Everyday food shopping: the north-east and the south-east

One shop in ten there is a grocery shop, and butchers are almost twice as present as elsewhere. It is the group with the most squares (287) and the fewest chains: 10.3% of shops, less than half the share of the fashion streets. Clothing weighs half the average.

The 19th (97% of its shops) and the 20th (93%) are almost entirely in it, the 18th for two thirds (70%), the 12th and the 13th for half (51%).

4. Neighbourhood services: the west and the south-west

Here, services for the person and the home stand out: beauty salons (11.4% against 7.2%), dry cleaners, cobblers, florists. People go out less there: bars and cafés are almost half as present as the average. Chains are numerous (20.4%), twice the share found in everyday food shopping.

Three arrondissements hold 70% of the group’s shops: the 15th, the 16th and the 17th. It covers 77% of the shops of the 16th, 67% of the 15th, 61% of the 17th and 59% of the 7th.

What changes from one group to another, beyond the types

Two things, however, do not change. The share of shops opened since 2020 is almost the same everywhere, between 42.6% and 45.8%: no group renews itself clearly faster than the others. And outside the centre no arrondissement is a single block: the 14th splits into three nearly equal thirds between neighbourhood services, everyday food shopping and going out.

Why four groups, and not six

The method (k-means, a classic of statistics) always returns the number of groups it is asked for. Nothing in the data imposes a number: Paris is a gradient, not a mosaic. So we looked for the number of groups that holds when the grid is moved. If every square is shifted by 125 metres, a shop should stay in the same group.

Our first attempt classed each square from its own shops alone, into six groups. It gave fine names, and it did not hold: shifted by 125 metres, more than one shop in two changed group (44.4% stayed). A 250-metre square has a few dozen shops spread over 28 types; that is too few for its mix to be anything but partly chance.

Describing each square with its eight neighbours, about 750 metres across, changes the result: with four groups, 86.2% of shops keep their group when the grid is shifted, 83.6% with 200-metre squares and 87.7% with 300-metre squares. The edges between groups move; the blocks stay.

What these groups leave out must also be said. They account for 34.6% of the differences in mix between squares. The remaining two thirds belong to each street.

What these figures do not say

  • The names are ours. The computer returns numbered groups; “fashion streets” or “neighbourhood services” are a reading, not a result.
  • A group is an average. A square of “everyday food shopping” may hold a street of restaurants. The group describes about 750 metres around the square, not an address.
  • The edges are blurred. About one shop in seven changes group when the grid moves: a square on the border of two blocks may belong to either.
  • The choices are ours: 250 m squares, at least 20 open shops, eight neighbours, four groups. The squares kept hold 94% of the 60,189 open shops; streets with few shops are not on the map.
  • A shop is “open” according to the register. Closures reach it late, and sometimes never.
  • The type comes from the activity code the operator declares, and sole traders with no shop sign are not counted. Same shops and same rules as the animated map.
  • The mix of shops says nothing about customers. Incomes, footfall, rents: these groups take none of them into account.

And around your address?

A group describes a piece of the city. Pignon reads the same register around one precise address: the shops of your type within a few minutes’ walk, the residents, the footfall. These are signals, with the number and the source behind each.
